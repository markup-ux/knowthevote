Crime

The FBI tables are on the Crime Data Explorer. Persons Arrested, 2025 contains Table 69, Table 29, and Table 43. Offenses Known to Law Enforcement, 2025 contains Table 8. Expanded Homicide Tables, 2025 contains Table 4 and Table 6.

These maps are drawn on this page. The numbers are the FBI's. The map opens on fraud arrests from Crime in the United States, 2025, Table 69: arrests by state for agencies that sent 12 months of data. Indigo measures are the FBI's estimated offenses known to law enforcement in 2025, for the 50 states and the District of Columbia. That file is the Summary Reporting System estimates on the Crime Data Explorer, last modified August 2026. A darker indigo is a higher offense rate. On the population map above, purple is a count. A darker orange is a higher arrest rate.

The rate is the count divided by the population for that state, times 100,000, for 2025. On an orange map, that population is the one printed for the state in Table 69. On an indigo map, it is the population printed on the FBI's offense row. The population map above uses the Census Bureau's July 1, 2025 estimate.

Violent crime is murder and nonnegligent manslaughter, rape, robbery, and aggravated assault. Property crime is burglary, larceny-theft, and motor vehicle theft. Rape uses the definition the FBI adopted in 2013. Arson has no estimate in this file. Fraud, forgery and counterfeiting, and embezzlement are the first measures. Those three maps are Table 69 arrests, in orange. The other measures are estimated offenses, in indigo. Where an agency sent 3 to 11 months, the FBI estimated the missing months from that agency's reports. Where an agency sent fewer than 3 months, the FBI estimated from similar places in the state. The count on an indigo map is offenses known to law enforcement.

The FBI's summary of this release says estimated violent crime fell 9.3 percent from 2024 to 2025, and estimated property crime fell 12.4 percent. It says 17,075 agencies sent data, covering 96.2 percent of the population, and that the figures are as of April 2, 2026. The 2024 figures in the file were updated for this release. The file notes that limited data for 2025 were available for Florida and North Dakota. The District of Columbia row includes offenses reported by the Metro Transit Police and the District of Columbia Fire and Emergency Medical Services Arson Investigation Unit.

Sexual assault

Rape in these FBI tables uses the definition the FBI adopted in 2013. Rose is the estimated rape rate per 100,000 people in 2025, from the same offense file as the crime map. A darker rose is a higher rate. Orange is rape arrests per 100,000 people in 2025, from Table 69 of Crime in the United States: arrests by state for agencies that sent 12 months of data. A darker orange is a higher rate. That table is not a national estimate.

The FBI estimates 123,332 rape offenses in the United States in 2025, and 18,729 rape arrests. The arrest estimate is Table 29. An arrest in the Uniform Crime Reporting program is an arrest, a citation, or a summons. The sheet for arrests by race titles a column Offense charged. That is the offense on the arrest. It is not a charge a prosecutor filed, and it is not a conviction.

The estimated offense file has no race. Table 43 records the race an agency wrote down for a person who was arrested. It does not record the race of people in reports that did not end in an arrest. These tables cannot show that one group is reported and another is arrested. A bar longer than the Census mark is a difference between an arrest share and a Census race-alone share. It is not a measure of who commits an offense.

Table 43 covers 14,108 agencies and an estimated population of 313,186,729. The bar is the share of arrests in that table. Every row uses the same orange, the color of the arrest map. These are not the only chemicals that can be in fast food. Each group is one published study. A percent in one group is not comparable to a percent in another.

Edwards and coauthors bought 64 foods in San Antonio in 2017 and 2018 and measured 11 plasticizers. A hamburger with toppings had cheese, tomato, pickles, onion, lettuce, and condiments. A plain patty had none of those. Chicken burritos were ordered two ways: white rice, black beans, cheese, mild salsa, and lettuce; or refried pinto beans, cheese, onion, sour cream, tomato, lettuce, and a tomato sauce. The supplement marks six chains as the ones in the study: McDonald's, Burger King, Pizza Hut, Domino's, Taco Bell, and Chipotle. The result tables call them Chain A and Chain B and do not say which name had the higher amount.

Aggression and irritability

These studies asked whether a food chemical is associated with aggression. Evidence that one chemical causes aggression in people is limited. A study of hyperactivity or irritability is not a study of aggression. None of these studies tested the restaurants drawn on the map.

What was studied What the study found Limit
Trans fat Golomb and coauthors, 2012, studied 945 adults who completed a diet survey, out of 1,018 people screened. Higher dietary trans fat was associated with more aggression and irritability. The analysis adjusted for sex, age, education, alcohol, and smoking. PLOS One. The study observed the association. It did not assign people to eat trans fat, and the authors say confounding remains a concern. In 2015 the FDA determined that partially hydrogenated oils, the main source of artificial trans fat, are not generally recognized as safe. For most uses, manufacturers could not add them to food after June 18, 2018. Food already made could continue through distribution until January 1, 2020. FDA.
Artificial food colors and sodium benzoate McCann and coauthors, 2007, gave 153 children age 3 and 144 children age 8 or 9 a drink. The drink contained a mixture of artificial colors and sodium benzoate, or a placebo. Some mixtures were followed by higher hyperactivity scores. The Lancet. The outcome was hyperactivity, not aggression. Each drink mixed several additives, so the trial does not say which additive produced the change. The European Food Safety Authority later called the evidence limited. The effect was small, it was not consistent across ages and mixtures, and the authority did not change the safety evaluation of those additives. EFSA.
Calcium propionate, a bread preservative Dengate and Ruben, 2002, gave 27 children bread with calcium propionate or a placebo. The children had already been selected because their behavior improved on a diet that removed additives. Average behavior scores were not significantly different. Relative to placebo, behavior worsened for 52% and improved for 19%. PubMed. The group was small and already selected. The authors describe irritability, restlessness, inattention, and sleep disturbance in some children. The paper does not report a finding on aggression.
Soft drinks Shi and coauthors, 2020, used school surveys from 64 countries. The surveys included 263,890 adolescents, ages 12 to 18. Students who reported more soft drinks also reported more physical fights. Frequent fighting meant three or more fights in the past 12 months. Each additional time per day was associated with an 11% higher likelihood of frequent fighting, with a 95% confidence interval of 10% to 13%, after adjustment. Nutrients. The surveys do not say whether sugar, caffeine, or another part of the drink explains the association. Students reported their own drinks and fights. Fast-food intake also rose with soft-drink intake. The study did not assign anyone to drink soda.
Sugar Wolraich, Wilson, and White, 1995, combined 16 reports covering 23 blinded studies. Each study compared a known amount of sugar with an artificial-sweetener placebo. For every behavior and cognition measure they pooled, the confidence interval included no effect. JAMA. The authors say a small effect, or an effect in a subset of children, cannot be ruled out. These were short trials of one dose. They are not a study of a child's usual diet over years.

Of these, the trans fat study is the one that measured aggression in adults. The color and preservative studies are about hyperactivity, irritability, or attention. The soft-drink study counts fights and does not name a chemical.

Water

These are active community water systems in EPA's Drinking Water System Search. A community system serves homes. The number is EPA's count of quarters with a Safe Drinking Water Act violation over the past three years. That count includes monitoring and reporting violations. It is not a measurement from one tap, and it is not a score this page calculated.

A health-based flag means EPA currently marks a violation of a maximum contaminant level, a maximum residual disinfectant level, or a treatment technique. Neighborhoods use the same OpenStreetMap names as the restaurant list. A neighborhood is matched to the community system that serves the most people among systems whose served city matches that neighborhood's city and state. EPA does not publish a separate result for the neighborhood.
