Chemicals measured

These are not the only chemicals that can be in fast food. Each group is one published study. A percent in one group is not comparable to a percent in another.

Edwards and coauthors bought 64 foods in San Antonio in 2017 and 2018 and measured 11 plasticizers. A hamburger with toppings had cheese, tomato, pickles, onion, lettuce, and condiments. A plain patty had none of those. Chicken burritos were ordered two ways: white rice, black beans, cheese, mild salsa, and lettuce; or refried pinto beans, cheese, onion, sour cream, tomato, lettuce, and a tomato sauce. The supplement marks six chains as the ones in the study: McDonald's, Burger King, Pizza Hut, Domino's, Taco Bell, and Chipotle. The result tables call them Chain A and Chain B and do not say which name had the higher amount.

Higher and lower amounts

This is not a ranking of the most harmful chain. No study on this page publishes that score. The fry survey is the only comparison that names chains. The plasticizer paper prints a higher amount and a lower amount and does not name the chains. The packaging study does not name chains. These are measurements in food or in packaging, not a list of chemicals a chain adds as ingredients.

Aggression and irritability

These studies asked whether a food chemical is associated with aggression. Evidence that one chemical causes aggression in people is limited. A study of hyperactivity or irritability is not a study of aggression. None of these studies tested the restaurants drawn on the map.

What was studied What the study found Limit
Trans fat Golomb and coauthors, 2012, studied 945 adults who completed a diet survey, out of 1,018 people screened. Higher dietary trans fat was associated with more aggression and irritability. The analysis adjusted for sex, age, education, alcohol, and smoking. PLOS One. The study observed the association. It did not assign people to eat trans fat, and the authors say confounding remains a concern. In 2015 the FDA determined that partially hydrogenated oils, the main source of artificial trans fat, are not generally recognized as safe. For most uses, manufacturers could not add them to food after June 18, 2018. Food already made could continue through distribution until January 1, 2020. FDA.
Artificial food colors and sodium benzoate McCann and coauthors, 2007, gave 153 children age 3 and 144 children age 8 or 9 a drink. The drink contained a mixture of artificial colors and sodium benzoate, or a placebo. Some mixtures were followed by higher hyperactivity scores. The Lancet. The outcome was hyperactivity, not aggression. Each drink mixed several additives, so the trial does not say which additive produced the change. The European Food Safety Authority later called the evidence limited. The effect was small, it was not consistent across ages and mixtures, and the authority did not change the safety evaluation of those additives. EFSA.
Calcium propionate, a bread preservative Dengate and Ruben, 2002, gave 27 children bread with calcium propionate or a placebo. The children had already been selected because their behavior improved on a diet that removed additives. Average behavior scores were not significantly different. Relative to placebo, behavior worsened for 52% and improved for 19%. PubMed. The group was small and already selected. The authors describe irritability, restlessness, inattention, and sleep disturbance in some children. The paper does not report a finding on aggression.
Soft drinks Shi and coauthors, 2020, used school surveys from 64 countries. The surveys included 263,890 adolescents, ages 12 to 18. Students who reported more soft drinks also reported more physical fights. Frequent fighting meant three or more fights in the past 12 months. Each additional time per day was associated with an 11% higher likelihood of frequent fighting, with a 95% confidence interval of 10% to 13%, after adjustment. Nutrients. The surveys do not say whether sugar, caffeine, or another part of the drink explains the association. Students reported their own drinks and fights. Fast-food intake also rose with soft-drink intake. The study did not assign anyone to drink soda.
Sugar Wolraich, Wilson, and White, 1995, combined 16 reports covering 23 blinded studies. Each study compared a known amount of sugar with an artificial-sweetener placebo. For every behavior and cognition measure they pooled, the confidence interval included no effect. JAMA. The authors say a small effect, or an effect in a subset of children, cannot be ruled out. These were short trials of one dose. They are not a study of a child's usual diet over years.

Of these, the trans fat study is the one that measured aggression in adults. The color and preservative studies are about hyperactivity, irritability, or attention. The soft-drink study counts fights and does not name a chemical.
